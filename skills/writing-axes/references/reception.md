# Reception: the before-publication check

**When:** the document is about to enter a public venue where readers respond — aggregators, forums, mailing lists, social feeds, review queues. Run it after the review pass and together with title drafting (Gate 1); the title and the forecast constrain each other. Skip it for text with a known, bounded readership.

**The gap it covers:** responses to a public text divide into readings of the text and responses to what the text is attached to. The review workflow stress-tests the first class — the hostile expert attacks the claims, and rule 9 of the Argument axis in `axes.md` writes toward the smartest objector. In an open venue the claims are one surface among several, and a thread can fill entirely with material the text never asserted: the topic's standing controversies, the project's costs, one word in the title, the genre of the post itself.

**Why introspection misses it:** the writer's list of known weaknesses is generated from inside the project, and the curse of knowledge runs on criticism exactly as it runs on clarity (`sources/reader-research.md`, item 11): introspection cannot simulate the reader who arrives without the writer's frame. Forecasting by introspection pre-empts the weaknesses the writer can see while the thread runs on surfaces the writer never considered. Forecast by enumeration instead.

## The surfaces

Walk each; write down the readings it predicts. Most surfaces predict one or two. The point is coverage, not volume.

1. **Inherited debates.** Every load-bearing noun carries the venue's standing controversies, and they re-run in the thread whether or not the text touches them. The instrument is the venue's own archive: search it for the document's nouns and read the top prior threads. Their recurring responses are the forecast baseline — the one part of reception that is directly measurable in advance.
2. **Externalities.** Readers audit what the thing consumes, emits, or displaces — energy, materials, waste, privacy, labor, attention — whether or not the text raises them. List the inputs and outputs; each is a predicted reading.
3. **Contested vocabulary.** The word a hostile reader will litigate, usually in the title, sometimes in the first line. Evaluative words are the usual carriers: "original", "simple", "secure", "fair", "open". Find it and decide deliberately — defend it where it first appears, replace it, or accept the fight. Never leave it undefended by accident.
4. **Genre and premise.** Venue regulars parody the venue's own genres, and "why is this a post" is a reading of the form, not the content. Predict it from how low-effort responses to this genre usually run. It lands in **accept** below almost every time, because defending the premise in advance is reassurance about the thing being reassured (universal rule 15).
5. **The writer.** A public text is also read as a claim about its author — affiliation, motive, competence, self-promotion. When the author is exposed on this surface, the document has a Standing component: open the Standing section of `axes.md` and treat that component under its rules.
6. **Open basic questions.** The sincere question the title and opening leave unanswered, usually about mechanism or operation. These are not attacks. They are the cheapest findings in the check and the ones most worth folding into the text.

## Triage

Every predicted reading gets exactly one of three decisions. The hedge budget (universal rule 14) governs the whole exercise: folding every forecast into the text produces the disclaimed, defensive prose rules 13–15 exist to prevent, and a check that bloats the document has failed.

- **Fold** — address it in the text. Reserved for three cases: an open basic question; a contested word worth defending; and the one or two readings that would otherwise own the thread. For that last case the research is on file — revealing the negative material first measurably reduces its impact, and pre-exposure in weakened form builds resistance to the strong form arriving later (`sources/standing-social.md`, items 20–21, via `standing.md` rule 7). Attach the fold to the specific claim or word it protects, once.
- **Prepare** — write the reply now and hold it. The default for inherited debates and externality audits in venues with comments: the reading may never appear, and a prepared reply costs the text nothing while a folded one costs every reader a paragraph.
- **Accept** — decide in advance not to engage. Genre parody, premise attacks, and most low-effort readings. Writing the acceptance down is the point: it converts a future provocation into a decision already made.

## Hand back to the writer

Which criticisms to concede in public, and which fights to have, are decisions about the writer's public standing, not writing decisions. Surface each fold candidate with a recommendation, then execute what the writer chooses (universal rules 21–23).

## Composition with the lens fan-out

For high-stakes publications, the surfaces slot into the review workflow's optional subagent fan-out as additional lenses beside the named reader, the naive reader, and the hostile expert: a topic-adjacent specialist who knows the domain's controversies and ignores the claims; an externality auditor; a venue regular who pattern-matches the genre. Each returns predicted readings rather than defects. Consolidate in the same single pass.
