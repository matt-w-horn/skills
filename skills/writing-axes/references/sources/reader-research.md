## 8. Diátaxis (Daniele Procida, diataxis.fr)
Four modes and orientations (current live site): **tutorials** = learning-oriented; **how-to guides** = task/goal-oriented; **reference** = information-oriented; **explanation** = understanding-oriented. **Verbatim:** "The core idea of Diátaxis is that there are fundamentally four identifiable kinds of documentation, that respond to four different needs." On what goes wrong when modes are mixed (**Verbatim example**): "Often, writers of tutorials who are anxious that their students should know things overload their tutorials with distracting and unhelpful explanation." The framework's claim is that conflating the four (e.g., reference-style specs inside a tutorial, or step-by-step instructions inside an explanation) degrades all of them. The site is actively revised; re-check before quoting. Locator: diataxis.fr/start-here; diataxis.fr/tutorials.

## 9. Ginny Redish, *Letting Go of the Words* (2007; 2nd ed. 2012)
**Verbatim** (Redish's own site): "Letting Go of the Words, 2nd edition, is about creating great content for websites, mobile apps, and social media by thinking of **content as conversation**." The table of contents confirms "Good web writing is like a conversation" and "Good web writing answers people's questions." Her method of writing out the reader's questions in the order asked is **verified** via the chapter guidance (**Verbatim** TOC/section): "Ask questions as headings when people come with questions… Make them the questions people come with… Ask the question from the site visitor's point of view." Locator: redish.net/books/letting-go-of-the-words; catdir.loc.gov TOC.

## 10. Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*
**Trunk test — Verbatim** (end of ch. 6): "Imagine that you've been blindfolded and locked in the trunk of a car, then driven around for a while and dumped on a page somewhere deep in the bowels of a Web site. If the page is well designed, when your vision clears you should be able to answer these questions without hesitation…" (the questions: What site is this? What page am I on? What are the major sections? What are my options at this level? Where am I in the scheme of things? How can I search?). **Happy talk — Verbatim:** "Happy talk is the introductory, content-free text that's supposed to welcome users to a site and tell them how great it is… You can identify happy talk by the tiny voice in the back of your head saying, 'Blah, blah, blah…'" **Editions:** 2000 (1st), 2005 (2nd), *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited* 2014 (3rd). Locator: howtoes.blog summary; woodsnotes.wordpress.com.

## 11. Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014), ch. 3
**Verbatim:** "The curse of knowledge is the single best explanation I know of why good people write bad prose." On the remedy (**Verbatim**): "A better way to exorcise the curse of knowledge is to close the loop, as the engineers say, and get a feedback signal from the world of readers—that is, show a draft to some people who are similar to your intended audience and find out whether they can follow it." Pinker explicitly holds that introspection/empathy alone is insufficient because "we're not very good at figuring out what other people are thinking, even when we try really, really hard." This supports both parts of the claim: you cannot reliably introspect your way out, and showing a draft to real readers is the reliable remedy. (Pinker adds a secondary remedy: reread your own draft after enough time that it is no longer familiar.) Locator: davidlabaree.com (Pinker's Chronicle essay adapting ch. 3); psychologicalscience.org.

## 12. Jakob Nielsen / Nielsen Norman Group
**"How Users Read on the Web" (Oct 1, 1997) — Verbatim:** "People rarely read Web pages word by word; instead, they scan the page… In a recent study John Morkes and I found that 79 percent of our test users always scanned any new page they came across; only 16 percent read word-by-word." Recommendations (**Verbatim**): "the inverted pyramid style, starting with the conclusion" and "half the word count (or less) than conventional writing." **F-shaped pattern (2006) — Verbatim NN/g:** "Eyetracking visualizations show that users often read Web pages in an F-shaped pattern: two horizontal stripes followed by a vertical stripe." Current NN/g wording (2017–2020 updates) confirms the F-pattern persists but is one of several scanning patterns (layer-cake, spotted, commitment) and recommends front-loading and left-aligned cues. Locator: nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web; nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content.

## 29. "Progressive disclosure" — MISATTRIBUTED (corrected)
Not "Nielsen and Wilson." The concept originates with **John M. Carroll and colleagues at IBM's Watson Research Center in the early 1980s** (the "training wheels" interface and minimalist-instruction program). Earliest documented instance: **Carroll & Carrithers, "Blocking Learner Error States in a Training-Wheels System," *Human Factors* 26(4), Aug 1984 (doi 10.1177/001872088402600402)**. **Jakob Nielsen later popularized** the term and explicitly credits Carroll (**Verbatim**, NN/g): "In two pioneering studies, Dr. John M. Carroll and colleagues compared 'training wheels' designs… Carroll's training wheels studies provided early scientific support for the progressive disclosure design concept." **Verdict:** origin = Carroll/IBM (early 1980s); Nielsen = popularizer. Locator: nngroup.com/articles/training-wheels-user-interface; doi.org/10.1177/001872088402600402.
