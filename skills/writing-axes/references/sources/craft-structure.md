## 7. John McPhee, *Draft No. 4* (2017) — VERIFIED
**Verbatim:** "The lead—like the title—should be a flashlight that shines down into the story. A lead is a promise. It promises that the piece of writing is going to be like this." On structure (**Verbatim**): "they should not be imposed upon the material. They should arise from within it." Confirms both the flashlight metaphor and the position that structure is *derived from* the material. Locator: mihirchronicles.com/draft-number-four; Text Publishing book page.

## 13. John Swales, *Genre Analysis* (1990) — VERIFIED
**CARS model** (verbatim structure, summarizing Swales 1990): **Move 1 — Establishing a Territory** (claiming centrality; making topic generalizations; reviewing previous research); **Move 2 — Establishing a Niche** ("Four ways of doing this are identified: Counter-claiming; **indicating a gap**; question-raising; continuing of a tradition"); **Move 3 — Occupying the Niche** (outlining purpose/announcing present research; announcing findings; indicating article structure). The "indicating a gap" step is a Move-2 option — **verified**. **Discourse community — summary:** Swales defines it by six characteristics (broadly agreed common public goals; mechanisms of intercommunication among members; use of those mechanisms to provide information and feedback; one or more genres that further the community's aims; a specific lexis; and a threshold level of members with suitable expertise). Locator: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CARS_model; Swales, *Genre Analysis*, Cambridge UP, 1990.

## 14. Roy Peter Clark, *Writing Tools* (2006) — VERIFIED
**Gold coins — Verbatim** (Clark's tools list, Poynter/*Writing Tools*): "Place gold coins along the path. Don't load all your best stuff high in the story. Space special effects throughout the story, encouraging readers to find them and be delighted by them" (in the 50-tool blog list: "Reward the reader with high points, especially in the middle"). **Emphatic words at the end — Verbatim:** "Place strong words at the beginning of sentences and paragraphs, and at the end. The period acts as a stop sign. Any word next to the period says 'look at me.'" Locator: poynter.org/archive/2002/thirty-tools-for-writers; dcripe.files.wordpress.com/50-writing-tools.pdf.

## 17. William Zinsser, *On Writing Well* — VERIFIED
**Lead rule — Verbatim:** "The most important sentence in any article is the first one. If it doesn't induce the reader to proceed to the second sentence, your article is dead. And if the second sentence doesn't induce him to continue to the third sentence, it's equally dead. Of such a progression of sentences, each tugging the reader forward until he is hooked, a writer constructs that fateful unit, the 'lead.'" **Science-writing "ladder"/step-by-step — Verbatim:** "Imagine science writing as an upside-down pyramid. Start at the bottom with the one fact a reader must know before he can learn any more. The second sentence broadens what was stated first… so that you can gradually move beyond fact into significance and speculation." The governing principle is stated as "leading readers who know nothing, step by step, to a grasp of subjects they didn't think they had an aptitude for" (he even puns on the "next-highest rung… on the Darwinian ladder"). Locator: philology.science full text of ch. 15 ("Science and Technology"); nasw.org/article/zinssers-advice-science-writing.

## 23. Paul R. Halmos, "How to Write Mathematics" (1970) — VERIFIED
**Notation — Verbatim** (§16, "Use symbols correctly"): "it is good to use a symbol so consistently that its verbal translation is always the same." General rule (**Verbatim**): "never start a sentence with a symbol." (He also urges punctuating symbolic sentences like verbal ones and not overworking a small mark.) **Spiral method — Verbatim** (§6, "Write in spirals"): "According to the spiral plan the chapters get written and rewritten in the order 1, 2, 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 4, etc." And the spiral plan of *organization* (**Verbatim**): introduce a concept, do right by it, then "review Section 1, as nearly completely as possible, from the point of view of Section 2." Original venue: *L'Enseignement Mathématique* 16 (1970); reprinted in the AMS/MAA volume *How to Write Mathematics*. Locator: scott-nelson.net/readings/Halmos1973.pdf; e-periodica.ch (*L'Enseignement Mathématique*, 1970, vol. 16).

## 25. Joshua Schimel, *Writing Science* (2012, OUP) — VERIFIED
**OCAR — Verbatim** (Schimel's site and book): "In every story (whether fiction, journalism, or science) there are four critical elements: Opening, Challenge, Action, and Resolution." The book's chapters confirm the structure (The Opening; The Funnel: Connecting O and C; The Challenge; Action; The Resolution). **Hedging — summary:** Schimel advises calibrated hedging — qualify claims where the evidence warrants caution, but avoid diluting the paper's central message with reflexive or excessive qualification; confidence should track the strength of the result. Locator: schimelwritingscience.wordpress.com; wp.optics.arizona.edu PDF of *Writing Science*.

## 32. The "So what?" test — LARGELY UNATTRIBUTABLE CRAFT FOLKLORE
No single named journalist, style guide, or j-school text can be credited with the "So what?" test as usually described (asking, after each paragraph, what question the reader now has). It is taught broadly in journalism and technical-writing pedagogy as anonymous craft lore. The closest **documented** cousin is Gerald Graff & Cathy Birkenstein's *They Say / I Say* (2006), which popularized "So what? Who cares?" — but that is a test of a thesis's *significance*, not the per-paragraph reader-question drill. **Verdict:** genuinely unattributable as usually described. Locator: Graff & Birkenstein, *They Say / I Say* (Norton, 2006), ch. on "So What? Who Cares?"

## 33. Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle* — VERIFIED WITH CORRECTION (dates genuinely conflict)
The publication history is tangled and sources disagree. Best-supported reconstruction: **first published 1978** as *The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking*; a **1981** Minto International printing exists (Internet Archive); a major **1987** edition; and the expanded ***The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking, and Problem Solving* in 1996**, reissued 2002/2009. (Wikipedia gives 1985 for the first title and 1996 for the expanded — this **conflicts** with the widely cited 1978 origin; the conflict is reported rather than resolved.) **SCQA — summary of Minto's definition:** the introduction should tell a story in the order **Situation → Complication → Question → Answer**, where Situation = the agreed, current context; Complication = what changed or created the problem; Question = the question the situation-plus-complication raises in the reader's mind; Answer = the governing thought, which becomes the apex of the pyramid. Locator: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Minto; archive.org/details/pyramidprinciple0000mint; Goodreads (1987 listing).

## Simon Peyton Jones, "How to Write a Great Research Paper" — VERIFIED IN CONVERSATION (outside the numbered report)
**Verbatim** (slides, Microsoft Research / Northeastern-hosted PDF): "Your paper should have just one 'ping': one clear, sharp idea"; "If you have lots of ideas, write lots of papers"; an idea is "a re-usable insight, useful to the reader." Also **Verbatim**: writing papers is "a primary mechanism for doing research (not just for reporting it)." His slides quote Brooks's "The Computer Scientist as Toolsmith" on novelty having no merit in design (see `rhetoric.md` item 16 for the corrected citation). Locator: microsoft.com/en-us/research/academic-program/write-great-research-paper/ (slides PDF and talk videos).

## Mensh & Kording, "Ten simple rules for structuring papers," *PLOS Computational Biology* 13(9): e1005619 (2017) — VERIFIED IN CONVERSATION (outside the numbered report)
Open access. **Verbatim** Rule 1: "Focus your paper on a central contribution, which you communicate in the title." **Verbatim** Rule 2: "Write for flesh-and-blood human beings who do not know your work" — with the point that "you are also the world's least qualified person to judge your writing from the perspective of the naïve reader." Rule 3 is the context-content-conclusion (C-C-C) scheme at document, section, and paragraph scale; Rule 4 covers avoiding zig-zag ("Only the central idea of the paper should be touched upon multiple times") and using parallelism ("Resist the temptation to use a different word to refer to the same concept—doing so makes readers wonder if the second word has a slightly different meaning"); Rule 5 is "Tell a complete story in the abstract"; Rule 6 is the introduction as a funnel of progressively narrower gaps.
**Corrections on file:** C-C-C is Rule 3 (not 6), the abstract is Rule 5, the introduction gap-funnel is Rule 6 (not 7); "roughly four sentences" for the abstract is not theirs — they specify the C-C-C structure, not a length. Their reference list misprints Gopen & Swan's first author as "George GD"; the correct author is George D. Gopen. Locator: journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619.
