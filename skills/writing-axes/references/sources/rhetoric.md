## 4. Stephen Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument* (1958)
Six elements: claim (C), data/grounds (D), warrant (W), backing (B), qualifier (Q), rebuttal (R). **Summary of Toulmin's layout** (corroborated by a faithful secondary source quoting the model): "The argument may have a modal qualifier (Q), such as 'necessarily' or 'presumably', which explicates the force of the warrant. If the warrant does not provide necessity, its conditions of exception or rebuttal (R) may be noted." The qualifier **is attached to a specific claim** and expresses the *degree of force* of that claim; the rebuttal states circumstances that would defeat it. **Caveat:** Toulmin's primary text is largely paywalled; the wording above is from a faithful secondary rendering (arXiv math/0504090), not the 1958 book itself. Locator: arxiv.org/pdf/math/0504090.

## 5. Kenneth Burke, *Permanence and Change* (1935)
**Verbatim** (KB Journal, quoting P&C): a perspective by incongruity is a "deliberate misfit"; it "appeals by exemplifying relationships between objects which our customary rational vocabulary has ignored" (PC 90). Also **Verbatim**: "planned incongruity should be deliberately cultivated for the purpose of experimentally wrenching apart all those molecular combinations of adjective and noun, substantive and verb, which still remain with us" (PC 119). Burke frames the productive value as arising precisely from the mismatch — the metaphorical "wrenching loose" of a word from its customary category into a new one (a point he sharpens in *Attitudes Toward History* as "metaphorical extension"). **Verified.** Locator: kbjournal.org/long_tending; kbjournal.org/anders.

## 6. Larry McEnerney, "The Craft of Writing Effectively"
**Verbatim** (transcript, Singju Post): "experts use language in one set of patterns to do their thinking, but those very same experts read with a different pattern." His core thesis is that a text's value lies with readers, not in the text or in the writer's ideas, and that clarity/organization/persuasiveness rank *below* value ("If it's organized and useless, it's useless. If it's persuasive and useless, it's useless"). He uses the term **"instability"** for the problem a paper must address: the writer must make an instability visible, then show the cost of that instability (or the benefit of resolving it) to the reader. Transcript source (identified): singjupost.com/the-craft-of-writing-effectively-larry-mcenerney-transcript (transcript of the University of Chicago Leadership Lab lecture).

## 15. Chip & Dan Heath, *Made to Stick* (2007)
**Sinatra Test — Verbatim** (ch. 4, "Credible"): "An example passes the Sinatra Test when one example alone is enough to establish credibility in a given domain. For instance, if you've got the security contract for Fort Knox, you're in the running for any security contract… If you catered a White House function, you can compete for any catering contract. It's the Sinatra Test: If you can make it there, you can make it anywhere." **Testable credentials — Verbatim:** "This challenge—asking customers to test a claim for themselves—is a 'testable credential.' Testable credentials can provide an enormous credibility boost, since they essentially allow your audience members to 'try before they buy.'" Named example: Wendy's 1984 "Where's the Beef?" campaign. Locator: faculty.bard.edu *Made to Stick* intro PDF; engineerguy.com/white-papers/made-to-stick.htm.

## 24. Ken Hyland, *Hedging* (1998) & *Metadiscourse* (2005) [Caveat]
**Functional account — Verbatim** (*Hedging in Scientific Research Articles*, preface): hedges are "employed to achieve a single primary objective: to overcome the inherent negatability of statements and gain the reader's acceptance of a knowledge claim," and in doing so "can emphasise an orientation to either the proposition or the reader." Also **Verbatim:** "Hedging is central to academic writing as it expresses possibility rather than certainty and collegiality rather than presumption." **Limitation (explicit):** Hyland was **not** found asserting that *excessive* hedging shifts reader attention from the claim to the author's confidence. His model distinguishes **content-oriented** vs. **writer-oriented** hedges (the latter minimize author accountability), but he presents hedging as functional and community-normed, not as a documented, empirically-measured attention-shifting harm. **That specific claim is therefore NOT supported in Hyland's work as located** — do not attribute it to him. Locator: api.pageplace.de preview PDF (Benjamins, PB&NS 54); benjamins.com/catalog/pbns.54.

## 28. Stasis theory [Caveat]
Hermagoras of Temnos (2nd c. BCE) is conventionally credited, but his *Technē* is **lost**, surviving only in later summaries — so a writer should not cite a Hermagoras primary text. **Primary sources to cite:** Cicero, *De Inventione* I.viii (the four questions: fact / name / class / action) and Quintilian, *Institutio Oratoria* III.vi; for the Greek tradition, Hermogenes, *On Staseis*. **Mapping of the four stases:** conjecture/fact (Lat. *coniectura*; Gk. *stochasmos*), definition (*definitio*; *horos*), quality (*qualitas*; *poiotēs*), and procedure/objection or jurisdiction (the "translative" stasis, *translatio* — proper venue/procedure). **Verbatim** (BYU Rhetoric, summarizing the tradition): "Four such basic kinds of conflict were categorized by the Greeks and Romans: conjectural, definitional, qualitative, and translative." **Recommended citation form:** attribute the scheme to "Hermagoras, as reported by Cicero (*De Inventione*) and Quintilian (*Institutio Oratoria*)." Locator: rhetoric.byu.edu/Canons/Invention/Stasis.htm; pressbooks.calstate.edu *Writing Arguments in STEM*.

## 30. Speech act theory (Austin 1962; Searle 1969) [Caveat]
**Illocutionary force — summary** (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy; Searle): the *illocutionary force* of an utterance is what the utterance *counts as doing* — asserting, promising, ordering, warning — as distinct from its propositional content (symbolized F(p)). "Force" answers the question "what is the force of those words?" (a threat? a prediction? a command?). Austin, *How to Do Things with Words* (1962); Searle, *Speech Acts* (1969); Searle & Vanderveken (1985) define force via seven components (illocutionary point, degree of strength, mode of achievement, and content/preparatory/sincerity conditions plus sincerity strength). **Separate question — does an established writing-craft source apply speech-act analysis as a sentence-level revision technique ("ask what each sentence does to the reader")?** **No** canonical writing-craft text was found that formally imports speech-act theory as a revision heuristic. Adjacent ideas exist (Klinkenborg's "know what each sentence says/implies"; McEnerney on what writing *does* to readers), but neither invokes Austin/Searle. **Conclusion: that heuristic is craft folklore, not traceable to speech-act theory.** Locator: plato.stanford.edu/entries/speech-acts; cambridge.org (Searle, *Expression and Meaning*, ch. 1).

## 36. Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, Book I
**Bekker citation:** 1356a (*Rhetoric* I.2, ~1356a1–13). **Translation quoted:** J. H. Freese (Loeb), via Perseus. **Verbatim:** "The first [proof] depends upon the moral character of the speaker… But this confidence must be due to the speech itself, not to any preconceived idea of the speaker's character; for it is not the case, as some writers of rhetorical treatises lay down in their 'Art,' that the worth of the orator in no way contributes to his powers of persuasion; on the contrary, moral character, so to say, constitutes the most effective means of proof." This directly supports the claim that ethos is produced **by the speech itself**, not by prior reputation. **Translation caveat:** wording varies substantially — Kennedy renders the key point as ethos achieved "by the speech… not… by a previous opinion that the speaker is a certain kind of person," and Roberts differs again. Cite whichever translation is quoted. Locator: perseus.tufts.edu, *Rhetoric* Bekker page 1356a (Freese trans.).

## Wayne C. Booth, "The Rhetorical Stance," *College Composition and Communication* 14(3): 139–145 (1963) [Conversation-verified]
Booth defines the rhetorical stance as a balance among the available argument about the subject, the interests of the audience, and the implied character of the speaker, demonstrated by contrast with three corruptions. The **pedant's stance** (summary, his framing): ignoring the audience or over-relying on the pure subject — "it consists of ignoring or underplaying the personal relationship of speaker and audience and depending entirely on statements about a subject." The **advertiser's stance — Verbatim:** it "comes from undervaluing the subject and overvaluing pure effect: how to win friends and influence people" (p. 143). The **entertainer's stance — Verbatim:** the "willingness to sacrifice substance to personality and charm" (p. 144). Locator: the essay in *CCC* 14(3); teaching editions at saskoer.ca and socialsci.libretexts.org quote the page-cited definitions.

## Richard Feynman, "Cargo Cult Science" (Caltech commencement, 1974) [Conversation-verified]
**Verbatim** (Caltech's own archive): "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself—and you are the easiest person to fool. So you have to be very careful about that. After you've not fooled yourself, it's easy not to fool other scientists. You just have to be honest in a conventional way after that." Also **Verbatim** on reporting obligations: "a specific, extra type of integrity that is not lying, but bending over backwards to show how you are maybe wrong." Locator: calteches.library.caltech.edu/51/2/CargoCult.htm.
