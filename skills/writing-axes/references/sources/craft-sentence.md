## 1. Gopen & Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing" (1990) — VERIFIED
**Verbatim** (USENIX-hosted full text; also tue.nl): "It is a linguistic commonplace that readers naturally emphasize the material that arrives at the end of a sentence. We refer to that location as a 'stress position.'" And: "The information that begins a sentence establishes for the reader a perspective for viewing the sentence as a unit: Readers expect a unit of discourse to be a story about whoever shows up first." On old/new information (**Verbatim**): "We refer to this familiar, previously introduced material as 'old information.' Conversely, material making its first appearance in a discourse is 'new information.' When new information is important enough to receive emphasis, it functions best in the stress position." Their key formulation, which they insist is a *principle, not a rule* (**Verbatim**): "Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize." Citation confirmed: *American Scientist* 78(6): 550–558 (Nov–Dec 1990). Locator: usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf.

## 2. Joseph M. Williams, *Style* — VERIFIED
**(a) Old-before-new.** Williams's own summary principles (**Verbatim**, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*, Williams & Bizup): "Open your sentences with information familiar to your reader" and "Push new, complex units of information to the end of the sentence." He teaches new meanings for the terms "topic" and "stress." **(b) Hedges/intensifiers — summary:** Williams treats hedges and intensifiers as legitimate devices whose problem is *volume and calibration*, not presence; he argues readers will "fill in the missing hedges" and warns against both over-hedging and over-intensifying. **Editions note:** first as *Style: Ten Lessons in Clarity and Grace*; later *Style: Toward Clarity and Grace* and *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*; the 13th edition is revised by Joseph Bizup, who reordered the lessons and retired Williams's first-person "I." Locator: clc.hcmus.edu.vn PDF (Williams & Bizup); Pearson 13th-ed. preface.

## 3. Verlyn Klinkenborg, *Several Short Sentences About Writing* (2012) — VERIFIED (with nuance)
**Verbatim** (Literary Hub excerpt; barnsworthburning extract): "Know what each sentence says, / What it doesn't say, / And what it implies. / Of these, the hardest is knowing what each sentence actually says." Also: "When the work is really complete, the writer knows how each sentence got that way." **Nuance:** the "know exactly what a sentence knows" idea is strongly supported by this material; but the specific proposition "every sentence should be able to stand alone" is best labeled a **summary** of his emphasis on the self-sufficient sentence — no single verbatim sentence in those words was located. Locator: lithub.com/verlyn-klinkenborg-on-writing-more-clearly.

## 18. Ursula K. Le Guin, *Steering the Craft* (1998; rev. 2015) — VERIFIED
Yes — the book contains exercises explicitly targeting **rhythm and syntax**, not just length. Named exercises: **Exercise One "Being Gorgeous"** (the sound/rhythm of prose); **Exercise Three "Short and Long"** (Ch. 3 is titled "Sentence Length and Complex Syntax" — one part demands sentences of ≤7 words, another a single sentence up to ~350 words); **Exercise Four "Again and Again and Again"** (repetition); **Exercise Five "Chastity"** (restraint with adjectives/adverbs). Locator: wiki.feministsf.net; thomasbooker.co.uk exercise walkthrough.

## 31. Gary Provost, "This sentence has five words…" — VERIFIED
Source confirmed: **Gary Provost, *100 Ways to Improve Your Writing* (1985)**, in the section "Vary Sentence Length." The passage is authentic to Provost and is frequently circulated online as an anonymous "writing tip" or misattributed to others — so the common decontextualized attribution is often wrong, but Provost is the correct author. (The full passage is not reproduced here; identifying markers are the opening "This sentence has five words" and the closing crescendo "the roll of the drums, the crash of the cymbals.") Provenance confirmed via Penguin Random House's listing for the updated edition (ISBN 9781984803689). Locator: penguinrandomhousesecondaryeducation.com.

## Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946) — VERIFIED IN CONVERSATION (outside the numbered report)
**Verbatim** (Orwell Foundation full text; corroborated by The Marginalian and Goodreads quote pages): "A scrupulous writer, in every sentence that he writes, will ask himself at least four questions, thus: What am I trying to say? What words will express it? What image or idiom will make it clearer? Is this image fresh enough to have an effect? And he will probably ask himself two more: Could I put it more shortly? Have I said anything that is avoidably ugly?" Followed by (**Verbatim**): "But you are not obliged to go to all this trouble. You can shirk it by simply throwing your mind open and letting the ready-made phrases come crowding in."
**Correction on file:** commonly garbled as "five questions" or merged with the six *rules*; it is four questions plus two optional ones, and the six rules are a separate list later in the essay. Locator: orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/.
