## 19. Scott & Lyman, "Accounts" (1968) — VERIFIED
**Verbatim** (abstract): "Accounts may be classified by content as excuses and justifications, each with its own subtypes. Excuses and justifications are socially approved vocabularies which neutralize an act or its consequences when one or both are called into question." Opening definition (**Verbatim**, p. 46): "An account is a linguistic device employed whenever an action is subjected to valuative inquiry." **Definitions (summary, corroborated by the Sage *Encyclopedia of Social Deviance*):** an **excuse** admits the act was bad/wrong but denies full responsibility; a **justification** accepts responsibility for the act but denies it was bad/wrong. Citation confirmed: *American Sociological Review* 33(1): 46–62. Locator: researchgate.net/publication/17542361_Accounts; sk.sagepub.com encyclopedia entry.

## 20. Williams, Bourgeois & Croyle, "Stealing Thunder" (1993) — VERIFIED WITH CORRECTION
Correct journal is **Law and Human Behavior, 17(6): 597–609 (1993), doi 10.1007/BF01044684** — *not* Basic and Applied Social Psychology. **Verbatim finding** (Springer abstract): "Stealing thunder is defined as revealing negative information about oneself (or, in a legal setting, one's client) before it is revealed or elicited by another person… In both studies, stealing thunder significantly reduced the impact of the negative information." (Two simulated-trial studies: a criminal assault trial and a civil negligence trial.) Locator: link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01044684.

## 21. William J. McGuire, inoculation theory (1961) — VERIFIED
The two foundational 1961 *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* papers: (1) **Papageorgis & McGuire (1961)**, "The generality of immunity to persuasion produced by pre-exposure to weakened counterarguments," 62(3): 475–481 (doi 10.1037/h0048430); (2) **McGuire & Papageorgis (1961)**, "The relative efficacy of various types of prior belief-defense in producing immunity against persuasion," 62(2): 327–337 (doi 10.1037/h0042026). Also **McGuire (1961)**, "Resistance to persuasion conferred by active and passive prior refutation of the same and alternative counterarguments," JASP 63(2): 326–332 (doi 10.1037/h0048344). **Core finding — Verbatim:** beliefs can be "'inoculated' against persuasion in subsequent situations involving forced exposure to strong counterarguments by pre-exposing the person to the counterarguments in a weakened form that stimulates—without overcoming—his defenses." **Citation caveat:** the frequently paired forewarning study is McGuire & Papageorgis (1962) in *Public Opinion Quarterly* 26: 24–34; a "Sociometry" citation is likely a confusion — McGuire's later synthesis appears in *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* (1964). Locator: communicationcache.com PDF; doi.org/10.1037/h0048344.

## 22. Erving Goffman, *Stigma* (1963) — VERIFIED
Goffman treats **"Passing"** (≈pp. 73–91, Prentice-Hall 1963) and **"Covering"** (≈pp. 102–103) as separate sections of Chapter 2. **Verbatim** (Goffman, via Wikipedia's sourced quotation): passing is "the management of undisclosed discrediting information about self." **Covering — summary** (from the primary text's structure and standard readings): covering is the effort of a person whose stigma is *already known or visible* to keep it from looming large in interaction — reducing its obtrusiveness — as distinct from **passing**, which conceals the stigma so it is not known at all. In short: passing hides the stigma; covering manages the tension of a stigma that is not hidden. Locator: avalonlibrary.net full-text PDF (Prentice-Hall 1963), Contents (Passing p.73, Covering p.102).

## 26. BLUF in US Army writing doctrine — VERIFIED WITH CORRECTION
The common citation to FM 6-22 is not the doctrinal home. The authoritative source is **AR 25-50, *Preparing and Managing Correspondence*** (current version dated 17 May 2013; publication ARN42124). **Verbatim** (AR 25-50, para 1-38, "Standards for Army writing"): "Effective Army writing is understood by the reader in a single rapid reading and is clear, concise, and well organized… Two essential requirements include putting the main point at the beginning of the correspondence (bottom line up front) and using the active voice." The concept was earlier articulated in the now-**rescinded** DA Pamphlet 600-67, *Effective Writing for Army Leaders* (1986), which coined "the standard for Army writing" and mandated BLUF because "the greatest weakness in ineffective writing is that it doesn't quickly transmit a focused message." (The original AR 600-70, *The Army Writing Program*, 1985, was superseded by AR 25-50 in 1988.) Locator: armypubs.army.mil ARN42124-AR_25-50 PDF; Military Review, "Effective Writing for Army Leaders."

## 27. Olivier / Hoffman "try acting" anecdote — MISATTRIBUTED / APOCRYPHAL (as usually told)
The quip is real and widely repeated ("Why don't you try acting, dear boy? It's much easier"), but the standard framing — Olivier mocking Hoffman's Method preparation on *Marathon Man* (1976) — is **disputed by Hoffman himself**. Per IMDb trivia and Austin Kleon (quoting Isaac Butler's *The Method*), Hoffman "repeatedly denied the story, and finally cleared up the matter in 2004": he explained the torture scene was shot early morning, he was going through a divorce and had been partying for two prior nights, and Olivier's comment addressed his *lifestyle*, not his acting technique. Screenwriter **William Goldman** (author of *Marathon Man*) called the story apocryphal. **Verdict:** an exchange likely occurred, but the popular "mocking Method" version is garbled and denied by the principal. **Technical vs. Method (summary):** the "technical"/British tradition (Olivier) builds character *outside-in* — voice, body, external craft — creating the *illusion* of feeling; Strasberg's **Method** works *inside-out* via affective/emotional memory to generate real feeling. Locator: imdb.com/title/tt0074860/trivia; austinkleon.substack.com.

## 35. Sanford Meisner, *Sanford Meisner on Acting* (1987) — VERIFIED
**Definition — Verbatim:** "Acting is living truthfully under imaginary circumstances" (attributed to p. 63). **Core of the technique — summary** (corroborated by Backstage and StudioBinder): attention is placed **outward, on the scene partner** rather than on oneself; emotion is generated by real-time response to the partner (via the Repetition / "Word Repetition Game" exercise), not by internal emotional recall — the key contrast with Strasberg's Method. Meisner's dictum that an actor should "not do anything until something makes them do it" captures the outward, reactive orientation. Locator: physicsofconnection.com/library/ref-meisner-on-acting; backstage.com Meisner guide.
